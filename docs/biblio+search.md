Key Documents in Behavioral Economics – Annotated Bibliography



Below is a curated list of seminal documents (mostly articles and essays under ~20 pages) that together provide a comprehensive overview of behavioral economics. These works range from foundational theory and classic experiments to surveys of the field and recent perspectives. Each entry includes a direct link to the document (PDF) and a brief description of its significance.



Simon (1955) – A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice. This classic paper by Herbert A. Simon introduces the concept of bounded rationality, arguing that real human decision-makers have limited cognitive resources and cannot achieve the fully rational optimization assumed in classical economics. Simon proposes that individuals satisfice (seek “good enough” solutions) rather than optimize, laying early groundwork for behavioral economics by formalizing why and how actual behavior might depart from strict rationality.



Tversky \& Kahneman (1974) – Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases. In this landmark Science article, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman demonstrate that people rely on mental shortcuts (heuristics) like representativeness, availability, and anchoring when making judgments under uncertainty. These heuristics simplify decisions but also lead to systematic biases and deviations from rational probability judgments. This work launched the “heuristics and biases” research program and revealed pervasive errors such as ignoring base rates, anchoring on irrelevant values, and overestimating small probabilities.



Kahneman \& Tversky (1979) – Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk. This foundational paper (published in Econometrica) presents Prospect Theory, a descriptive model of how people evaluate risky choices, challenging the expected utility model. Kahneman and Tversky show that individuals value gains and losses relative to a reference point rather than final wealth, exhibit loss aversion (losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good), and overweight small probabilities. They document effects like the certainty effect (risk-aversion in gains, risk-seeking in losses when outcomes are certain vs. probabilistic) and the isolation effect, explaining why preferences can reverse based on how options are framed. Prospect Theory became one of the central models in behavioral economics, explaining phenomena from insurance purchase to lottery play.



Tversky \& Kahneman (1981) – The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice. In this short Science article, the authors show that the way choices are framed (in terms of gains or losses) can dramatically shift preferences. They give the famous “Asian disease” problem: when lives saved are emphasized, people prefer a sure thing; when lives lost are emphasized (an objectively equivalent outcome), people prefer a risky gamble. This demonstrated framing effects and highlighted violations of descriptive invariance (a basic rationality requirement). The paper deepens Prospect Theory’s insight that reference points and wording influence decisions, underlining a core theme of behavioral economics: seemingly irrelevant changes in presentation can predictably alter choices.



Thaler (1980) – Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice. Richard Thaler’s influential article discusses several anomalies in consumer decision-making that depart from standard economic theory. He introduces ideas like the endowment effect (people value goods more when they own them than when they don’t), mental accounting (people treat money differently depending on arbitrary categories or budgets), and the pain of paying (how payment timing affects enjoyment). Thaler uses everyday examples and evidence to argue for a more “positive” (descriptive) theory of consumer choice that incorporates psychological insights. This paper helped spark interest in integrating psychology into economics and foreshadowed many later developments.



Kahneman, Knetsch \& Thaler (1991) – The Endowment Effect, Loss Aversion, and Status Quo Bias. This brief Journal of Economic Perspectives piece documents how people’s willingness to accept compensation to give up an item is often much higher than their willingness to pay to acquire it. The authors link this endowment effect to loss aversion – giving something up feels like a loss, which looms larger than a gain of the same size. They also discuss status quo bias, a preference to stick with one’s current situation. The article reports experiments (e.g. with mugs and lotteries) showing that these biases persist even with market experience. This accessible paper helped popularize these concepts and illustrated the profound implications of Prospect Theory for market behavior and consumer choice.



Thaler (1988) – Anomalies: The Ultimatum Game. In this JEP “anomalies” column, Thaler reviews findings from the ultimatum game – a simple bargaining experiment – that challenge the assumption of purely self-interested economic agents. In the game, one player proposes how to split a sum of money and the other can accept or reject (in which case both get nothing). The rational-selfish prediction is that any positive offer is accepted, but experiments showed people often reject “unfair” low offers and proposers tend to offer a substantial share (around 40-50%). Thaler discusses how preferences for fairness and willingness to punish unfairness (even at personal cost) are anomalies for standard theory but consistent with psychological utility from fairness. This work was key in introducing ideas of social preferences into economics and showed that real behavior in bargaining reflects norms of fairness, not just monetary payoff.



Tversky \& Kahneman (1992) – Advances in Prospect Theory: Cumulative Representation of Uncertainty. This paper refines Prospect Theory into Cumulative Prospect Theory, extending it to accommodate uncertain outcomes with many probabilities (not just simple two-outcome gambles). Tversky and Kahneman introduce cumulative weighting of probabilities and allow different weighting functions for gains and losses, while retaining the key ideas of a reference-dependent value function that is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses (reflecting loss aversion). They review new experimental evidence confirming the distinctive “fourfold pattern” of risk attitudes predicted by the theory (risk-averse for high-probability gains and low-probability losses; risk-seeking for low-probability gains and high-probability losses). This more general formulation solidified the Prospect Theory framework as the leading alternative to expected utility and has been widely applied in economics and finance.



Laibson (1997) – Golden Eggs and Hyperbolic Discounting. David Laibson’s influential paper introduces hyperbolic discounting to economics, providing a model of time-inconsistent preferences (published in QJE). He shows that unlike the standard exponential discounting, hyperbolic (or present-biased) discount functions—where people heavily discount immediate delays but are more patient over longer horizons—can explain behaviors like procrastination and lack of self-control in saving. Laibson’s model (with quasi-hyperbolic discounting) implies people will have a motive to constrain their own future choices (e.g. through commitment devices), aligning with intuitions about self-control problems. This work was seminal in bringing psychological realism to intertemporal choice, influencing models of savings, retirement, and credit use. (Link contains full paper.)



Mullainathan \& Thaler (2000/2001) – Behavioral Economics. This NBER working paper (and International Encyclopedia of Social \& Behavioral Sciences entry) offers a concise overview of behavioral economics up to 2000. Sendhil Mullainathan and Richard Thaler discuss the central idea that traditional economic models can fail when agents have “human” traits – bounded rationality, bounded willpower, and bounded self-interest. They explain each: bounded rationality refers to cognitive limitations on reasoning (people use heuristics and have framing effects); bounded willpower addresses time inconsistency and self-control problems (people may make choices that their long-run selves wouldn’t); bounded self-interest recognizes that people care about fairness and altruism, not only their own material payoff. The paper also illustrates applications in finance and savings behavior. This survey is an excellent introduction to the field, touching on key concepts and why markets don’t necessarily “fix” all human quirks (due to limits to arbitrage, etc.), and is written by two leaders of the discipline.



Rabin (1998) – Psychology and Economics. In this comprehensive Journal of Economic Literature survey, Matthew Rabin reviews how psychological findings can improve economic analysis. He catalogs a range of departures from the standard model – from judgment biases and overconfidence to reference dependence, fairness, and mistakes in statistical reasoning – and argues for integrating these into economics. Rabin’s article is highly cited for its clear exposition of where human behavior systematically diverges from “homo economicus” and how such insights can enrich economic theory and predictions. (The article is about 35 pages; if a direct PDF is needed, see the JEL or working paper version.)



Thaler \& Sunstein (2003) – Libertarian Paternalism Is Not an Oxymoron. In this short essay (AER Papers \& Proceedings), Thaler and legal scholar Cass Sunstein introduce the philosophy of “libertarian paternalism,” which underpins the concept of nudges. They argue that it is both possible and legitimate for institutions to steer people’s choices in welfare-promoting directions without eliminating freedom of choice. Because of cognitive biases and inertia, the way choices are designed (defaults, framing, etc.) can significantly influence outcomes – so policymakers might as well design choice architectures that help people achieve their own goals (e.g. higher savings rates via default enrollment in pensions). This piece laid the foundation for the popularization of nudging and behavioral public policy, reconciling freedom of choice with gentle guidance. (Link is to the 6-page AER version.)



Kahneman (2003) – Maps of Bounded Rationality: Psychology for Behavioral Economics. This is Daniel Kahneman’s Nobel Prize lecture (published in American Economic Review), where he reflects on the integration of psychology into economics. Kahneman summarizes decades of work (much of it with Tversky) on judgment and decision-making, including heuristics, biases, and Prospect Theory. He uses the metaphor of “maps” of bounded rationality to chart where human judgment deviates from the rational model. The lecture also discusses the two-system view of thought (intuitive vs. deliberative, a theme later popularized in Thinking, Fast and Slow). This article is a valuable, readable overview by one of the founding fathers of behavioral economics, covering key principles and experimental findings that earned him the Nobel. (Free PDF via AEA; about 25 pages.)



Thaler (2016) – Behavioral Economics: Past, Present, and Future. Richard Thaler’s AEA Presidential Address (published in American Economic Review, vol. 106(7)) provides a panoramic view of the field’s evolution. Thaler recounts how behavioral economics moved from the fringes to the mainstream, highlighting early challenges to the efficient markets and rational-agent paradigms, and how anomalies gradually gained acceptance. He emphasizes the need for descriptive models that account for human quirks, complementing normative models: “Expected utility theory remains the gold standard for how decisions should be made… Prospect theory… tells us how people actually make such choices.” He also points out open areas and the importance of evidence-based, inductive theorizing in economics. This address is an excellent high-level summary from a principal figure, bringing the reader up to the mid-2010s and discussing the then-emerging directions of research.



Thaler (2017) – Nobel Prize Lecture: From Cashews to Nudges. (Optional, not linked above) In 2017, Thaler received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his contributions to behavioral economics. His Nobel lecture (available via the Nobel Prize website) titled “From Cashews to Nudges” is a charming personal narrative of his key findings – from the famous “cashew moment” that illustrated self-control issues to the development of theories of mental accounting, fairness, and nudging. It serves as a user-friendly recap of how behavioral insights entered economics, reinforcing many themes present in the works listed above. (This can be found on Nobelprize.org and is a relatively short and accessible read.)



Each of these documents is freely accessible and provides insight into fundamental concepts or developments in behavioral economics. By reading through them, one can trace the arc from the early challenges to rationality assumptions, through the formation of core theories (heuristics-and-biases, prospect theory, etc.), to expansions into new domains (self-control, finance, policy), and up to reflections on the field’s current state and future. Together they offer a thorough grounding in behavioral economics – from its psychological underpinnings to its practical implications in the real world.
